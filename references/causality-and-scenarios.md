# Causality and scenarios

## Event-study discipline

1. Use the exact release timestamp and market timezone.
2. Determine whether the news was new, leaked, expected, or already priced.
3. Select windows appropriate to market liquidity: minutes or hours for liquid assets, days for slower transmission.
4. Measure price, yield, curve, spread, volume, volatility, and liquidity where relevant.
5. Check simultaneous data, speeches, auctions, earnings, geopolitical news, expiries, and technical breaks.
6. Compare control assets, adjacent maturities, currencies, countries, or sectors.
7. Separate announcement, implementation, settlement, and later macro effects.
8. Seek evidence that should exist if the proposed mechanism is true.

## Attribution register

| Candidate cause | Surprise | Timing fit | Mechanism fit | Supporting data | Contradiction | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use `confirmed mechanism`, `plausible contributor`, `market narrative`, or `contradicted`. A clean price move after a headline still may not identify causality when several events occur together.

## Scenario construction

Scenarios must differ by mechanism, not only percentage:

| Scenario | Trigger | Transmission | Asset / economy impact | Horizon | Leading indicator | Falsification |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Useful branches include:

- intended liquidity or policy effect remains localized;
- portfolio and leverage feedback amplify the move;
- offsetting issuance, inflation, growth, or policy dominates;
- implementation disappoints or the initial positioning reverses;
- repeated actions alter the policy or market regime.

## Winners and losers

Name the exposure rather than relying on broad labels:

- duration holder versus short-duration saver;
- fixed-rate borrower versus floating-rate borrower;
- importer versus exporter under a defined currency move;
- commodity producer versus consumer;
- leveraged balance sheet versus cash-rich balance sheet;
- domestic investor versus unhedged foreign investor;
- government financing cost versus household deposit income.

State first-order benefit, second-order risk, time horizon, and condition. The same participant can benefit immediately and lose later.
